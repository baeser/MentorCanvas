Program Overview

Supporting new educators through their first three years

🎯 Mission

The DCS New Teacher Mentor Program creates the conditions for new teachers to thrive — building confidence in practice, fostering well-being and belonging, deepening connection to our school community, and strengthening teaching and learning. We believe new teachers grow best through trusting, collaborative, strengths-based mentoring relationships that honor each educator's prior experience and individual goals.

🤝 The Mentor Role

Mentoring in DCS is non-supervisory, non-evaluative, and confidential. Mentors are not evaluators. The mentor's role is to support the new teacher's growth as a thought partner, sounding board, and trusted colleague — never to report on, judge, or assess them.

What's shared between mentor and mentee stays between them, with the only exceptions being situations involving student safety or clear ethical or legal concerns.

📓 Mentor–Mentee Contact Log

Every mentor pair maintains a contact log throughout the year. Each meeting is a row: date, start and end times, topic or activity, and both mentor and mentee initials.

Principal sign-off happens at two gate checks: November 15 for the first half (Aug–Nov) and May 1 for the second half (Dec–May), with year-end total hours filled in at the close.

📚 Teaching & Learning

Strengthen instructional practice through collaboration, observation, feedback, and reflection on what's working for students.

A Three-Year Journey

Per Michigan law (MCL 380.1526), new teachers receive mentoring across their first three years. Support is most intensive in Year 1 and gradually shifts toward independence.

1
Year 1: Foundation Orientation, routines, first observation cycles, and goal-setting. Most intensive support.
2
Year 2: Refinement Deepening practice, expanded peer observation, more autonomy in goal-setting.
3
Year 3: Independence Mentee-led growth with consultative mentor support; preparation for tenure and beyond.

The 90-Hour Professional Learning Requirement

A central piece of MCL 380.1526 — and the Mentoring & Induction Standards that flow from it.

⏱️ What the Law Requires

Michigan law requires every new teacher to receive at least 15 days (90 hours) of intensive professional learning across their first three years of classroom teaching, in addition to regular district professional development. First Aid and CPR training is required in Year 1 specifically.

These 90 hours are separate from the standard district professional development required of all teachers under MCL 380.1527 — hours cannot be double-counted.

📋 What Counts Toward the 90 Hours

The short answer: almost everything except district-provided professional development (the PD all staff attend on designated PD days). If it involves your mentee's professional growth and isn't a district PD session, it almost certainly counts.

•Mentor–mentee meetings focused on professional growth, observation debriefs, goal-setting, and reflection — tracked in the contact log
•Formal observations and post-observation conferences
•PLC time — collaborative planning and learning with colleagues counts
•Staff meetings with a professional learning component
•Department meetings focused on curriculum, instruction, or professional growth
•Regional or ISD-led seminars conducted by master teachers and mentors
•University-linked professional development school experiences
•First Aid and CPR training (required in Year 1 if not already certified)

What does NOT count: district-provided professional development required of all staff — those hours fall under MCL 380.1527 and cannot be double-counted toward the 90.

Mentor–mentee meeting time is logged in the contact log; other qualifying activities can be noted there as well. Together, all qualifying hours should total at least 90 by the end of Year 3.

Three Ways to Distribute the 90 Hours

There's no single right answer — the law specifies the total, not the year-by-year split. Pick the model that fits your mentee. Most pairs land somewhere between Model B and Model C.

MODEL A

Equal Time

30 / 30 / 30

Year 1: 30 hrs
Year 2: 30 hrs
Year 3: 30 hrs

Best for: Mentees who came in with strong preparation and don't need front-loaded support; also pairs that want a predictable, steady cadence.

Translation: ~1 hour per week (school weeks) across all three years.
MODEL B

Gentle Taper

35 / 30 / 25

Year 1: 35 hrs
Year 2: 30 hrs
Year 3: 25 hrs

Best for: Most pairs. Modestly more support in Year 1 while keeping Year 3 substantive enough to matter — recognizes growing independence without falling off a cliff.

Translation: Weekly Y1 → bi-weekly Y2 → bi-weekly Y3 with extra observations.
MODEL C

Front-Loaded

45 / 30 / 15

Year 1: 45 hrs
Year 2: 30 hrs
Year 3: 15 hrs

Best for: Mentees newer to the profession or to DCS who need intensive Year 1 support; also matches the natural intensity arc of the program design.

Translation: Weekly Y1 (~1.5 hrs/wk) → bi-weekly Y2 → monthly Y3.

💡 Within-Year Pacing

Whatever year-to-year split you choose, hours within each year tend to flow naturally from the program rhythm:

•Heavier: August (orientation), September (goal-setting & first observation), January (mid-year reset), and around gate checks (Nov 15, May 1)
•Lighter: December (semester closure) and April (testing season)
•Steady: October, February, March

The contact log's running total tells the real story — at the end of each year, you should be roughly on pace for whichever distribution model you chose.

✅ Mentor Eligibility

Any teacher who has earned tenure — or any previously tenured teacher who has completed a probationary period in DCS — may apply to become a mentor.

In addition, strong mentors bring:

•Strong content and pedagogical knowledge appropriate to their teaching role
•Skill working with adult learners — listening, asking questions, withholding judgment
•Capacity for self-reflection and responsiveness to a colleague's individual needs
•Willingness to complete required mentor training and ongoing professional learning

📋 Key Program Components

•Required mentor training before working with a mentee, with ongoing professional learning throughout the year
•Regular meetings at a frequency negotiated between mentor, mentee, and program leadership based on the mentee's experience and needs
•Goal-setting and progress review cycles co-developed early in the year and revisited regularly
•Formative observation cycles with non-evaluative feedback aligned to the mentee's goals
•Mentor peer collaboration — regular opportunities for mentors to share, problem-solve, and learn together
•Documentation — every meeting recorded in the DCS Mentor–Mentee Contact Log with date, start/end times, topic, and dual initials; building principal initials the log at two gate checks (Nov 15 for Aug–Nov, May 1 for Dec–May)
•Continuous program improvement — annual feedback from mentors and new teachers shapes the next year's program

August

Building the Relationship & Foundation

🌻

August Focus

August sets the tone for the entire mentoring relationship. The work this month is about establishing trust, learning who your mentee is as a person and a professional, orienting them to DCS, and beginning to co-develop goals. Resist the urge to front-load advice — listen first.

⚠️ A Reminder About Your Role

You are not your mentee's evaluator. Your role is non-supervisory, non-evaluative, and confidential. That distinction matters most in August, when first impressions of the relationship are formed. If your mentee senses that what they share with you might travel up the chain, they won't bring you the questions and struggles where you can actually help.

🤝 Relationship & Goals

Standard 7: Building a supportive, trusting partnership and beginning the goal-setting process.

•Have an initial conversation focused entirely on getting to know your mentee — their preparation pathway, prior experience, why they teach, what they're excited about, what they're worried about
•Exchange contact information and agree on preferred communication methods and reasonable response times
•Schedule recurring meetings for August and September at mutually convenient times — protect this time
•Explicitly discuss what confidentiality looks like in your relationship and reaffirm your non-evaluative role
•Begin draft goal-setting: invite your mentee to name 2–3 areas they want to grow in this year, drawn from their own sense of priorities (not from a checklist)
•Create a shared calendar or doc for tracking meetings, observations, and goal progress

💚 Well-Being & Belonging

Standard 8: Establishing sustainable practice and a sense of belonging from day one.

•Talk openly about work-life balance and the realistic intensity of the first weeks — normalize that exhaustion is common, not a sign of failure
•Help your mentee identify a "stop time" each evening and at least one full day per week with no school work
•Make introductions to other staff intentionally — focus on people who will help your mentee feel like they belong, not just people they need to know procedurally
•Share district employee assistance and wellness resources (don't assume your mentee knows where to find them)
•Mention professional organizations relevant to their content area and grade band

🌐 Culture & Community

Standard 9: Connecting your mentee to DCS, the building, and the Dexter community.

•Give a thorough building tour: mailboxes, restrooms, workroom, staff lounge, supply closets, copier, nurse, counseling office
•Introduce your mentee to building leadership, support staff, custodial staff, food service, and front office team
•Walk through entry/exit procedures, sign-in/out, alarm codes, parking, and outdoor/duty areas
•Share context on the Dexter community — who our families are, what they value, key traditions and rhythms of the school year
•Review district and building policies (sick leave, personal leave, professional days, dress, social media expectations)
•Discuss how to handle introductions to families — first contact tone, communication protocols

📚 Teaching & Learning

Standard 10: Supporting instructional practice from the start.

•Visit your mentee's classroom and offer hands-on setup help — physical space communicates a lot about who they want to be as a teacher
•Walk through technology: laptop, projector, document camera, phone/voicemail, Canvas, PowerSchool, email norms
•Talk through the first week of plans together — what they want students to experience and what they're hoping to establish
•Discuss confidentiality and FERPA — what student info can and can't be shared, and with whom
•Review where to find IEPs, 504 plans, and CA60s; discuss the mentee's responsibility for accommodations and how to ask for support from special ed staff
•Provide an overview of the DCS evaluation process and timeline — separate from your mentoring role, but important for them to understand
•Walk through MOECS and how to track professional learning (especially the 90-hour requirement for provisionally certified teachers)
💡 Mentor Tip: The most powerful thing you can do in August is listen more than you talk. Your mentee will get a flood of information from administration, IT, HR, and curriculum. Your job isn't to add to that flood — it's to help them make sense of it and feel like they have one trusted person in their corner.

📓 About the Mentor–Mentee Contact Log

Make your own copy of the contact log here: DCS Mentor–Mentee Contact Log →

Every time you meet with your mentee, fill in a row: date, start and end times, topics or activity, both your initials and your mentee's initials, and any optional follow-up notes. Total hours are tracked at the end of the year. The dual initials matter — they make clear the mentee was present and aware of what's logged, which reinforces the non-evaluative nature of the relationship.

Your building principal will initial the log at two points: once for the first half of the year (Aug–Nov) at the November 15 gate check, and again for the second half (Dec–May) at the May 1 gate check. Build the habit now: fill in the row right after each meeting, while it's fresh.

Hours add up across the three years. The contact log's running total contributes to the 90-hour requirement of MCL 380.1526 — see the Overview page for distribution models. By the end of Year 1, your log should reflect the pace you and your mentee chose.

September

Goal-Setting & First Observation Cycle

🍂

September Focus

August was about getting to know each other. September is about getting to work. Finalize the goals you started drafting last month, complete your first formative observation cycle, and establish the weekly rhythm of check-ins. The first few weeks reveal what the new teacher actually needs — adjust your support accordingly.

📌 This Month's Big Move: Complete a non-evaluative observation of your mentee and have a debrief conversation that's grounded in their stated goals — not your evaluative impressions. Ideally this happens before any administrator's formal observation.

🤝 Relationship & Goals

Standard 7: Finalizing goals and beginning the formative feedback cycle.

•Finalize 2–3 goals together — written, specific enough to observe, owned by the mentee
•Conduct an informal classroom observation focused on one of those goals (not a general evaluation)
•Hold a post-observation conversation that opens with the mentee's reflection, not your feedback — ask what they noticed before sharing what you noticed
•Confirm meeting cadence — weekly is appropriate for most Year 1 mentees, even if meetings are short
•Schedule October meetings before September ends
•Log every meeting and the observation cycle

💚 Well-Being & Belonging

Standard 8: Sustaining the new teacher through the first wave of fatigue.

•Check in honestly on energy and stress — late September is when the initial adrenaline fades and the workload starts to compound
•Watch for signs of overload: skipping meals, working past 9pm regularly, withdrawing from staff interactions, frequent illness
•Celebrate concrete wins — name specific things you've seen them do well; new teachers rarely get this from anyone else
•Help your mentee identify which parts of the workload are essential vs. perfectionistic — give them permission to do "good enough" on lower-stakes work

🌐 Culture & Community

Standard 9: Establishing classroom culture and integrating into the school community.

•Talk through emergency procedures: fire drills, lockdowns, weather emergencies, medical incidents
•Discuss the classroom culture your mentee is establishing — what's working, what feels off, what they want to adjust
•Walk through parent communication tools and DCS norms; review what kinds of communication go through which channels
•Continue staff introductions — by now your mentee should know names and roles of people they interact with regularly
•Mention upcoming building/district events (Homecoming, conferences, school board meetings) and what staff involvement typically looks like

📚 Teaching & Learning

Standard 10: Strengthening instructional practice through observation and feedback.

•Conduct your first observation cycle (see "Big Move" above) — focus on what your mentee asked you to look for
•Help your mentee build a strong substitute folder — emergency plans, seating chart, key procedures, who to ask for help
•Walk through state and district curriculum standards for their content area; discuss pacing
•Review classroom routines they've established — what's working, what students are struggling to follow
•Discuss the school discipline policy and intervention strategies before they're needed in a heated moment
•Revisit specific IEPs and 504s for students in their classes — accommodations are now in real practice, not theory
•Encourage early use of formative assessment to surface where students are, not where the teacher hopes they are
💡 Mentor Tip: A good post-observation conversation isn't a list of feedback — it's a conversation. Try opening with: "What did you notice about the lesson?" and "What were you watching the students do?" Their answers will tell you whether they need affirmation, a fresh strategy, or just time to think out loud.

📓 Logging Reminder

Log every meeting in the contact log: date, start/end times, topic, and both sets of initials. The November 15 gate check is six weeks away — if you log as you go, principal sign-off takes minutes. If you wait, it takes hours and you'll miss things.

October

Conferences, Community, & Refining Practice

🎃

October Focus

October is the first real test of the new teacher's stamina. The honeymoon is over, parent-teacher conferences are coming, and the lessons aren't always landing the way they did on paper. Your job this month is to help your mentee prepare for conferences, run a second observation cycle, and notice the early warning signs of burnout before they compound.

📌 This Month's Big Move: Walk your mentee through parent-teacher conference prep — including how to handle the difficult ones. A 20-minute role-play of a tough conversation will save them hours of anxiety and prevent at least one regrettable email.

🤝 Relationship & Goals

Standard 7: Sustaining the partnership and running the second observation cycle.

•Maintain weekly meetings; if you've slipped to every other week, get back on track now — November and December will erode the cadence on their own
•Conduct your second observation cycle, ideally focused on a different goal than September's
•Revisit the goals you set in September — are they still the right ones? It's normal for early goals to need revision once reality sets in
•Schedule November meetings; flag the November 15 Mentor Form deadline so it doesn't sneak up
•Continue logging every meeting and observation

💚 Well-Being & Belonging

Standard 8: Catching the first wave of burnout before it compounds.

•Ask directly: "What's the hardest part of your day right now?" — then listen without immediately problem-solving
•Watch for grading backlog — it's the most common source of October overwhelm; help your mentee triage what actually needs to be graded vs. what can be feedback-only
•Talk through the emotional weight of conferences — first-year teachers often catastrophize them; the reality is usually less dramatic than the anticipation
•Specifically name what you've seen them do well in the past month — your mentee may not be able to see their own progress
•Encourage at least one fully unplugged weekend day before conferences hit

🌐 Culture & Community

Standard 9: Family engagement and the rhythms of fall in DCS.

•Walk through parent-teacher conference logistics: scheduling, sign-up tools, building procedures, what to have prepared, where to keep notes
•Role-play a difficult conversation — a parent who disagrees, a parent who is upset, a parent whose child is struggling academically or behaviorally
•Coach on the structure of a productive conference: lead with strengths, name one growth area, share data, invite parent perspective, agree on a follow-up
•Discuss when to loop in administration or counseling — and when not to
•Share context on Dexter families — common community concerns, what shows up in conferences here that might not show up elsewhere
•Review school closure and weather communication protocols before the first delay or cancellation

📚 Teaching & Learning

Standard 10: Refining instruction based on what's actually happening with students.

•Run your second observation cycle; pick a focus area drawn from your mentee's stated goals or current pain points
•Look at student data together — formative assessments, early grades, attendance patterns — and help your mentee notice what the data is and isn't telling them
•Discuss differentiation for the students who are now clearly struggling or clearly ahead
•Revisit specific IEP and 504 accommodations — are they being implemented? what's getting in the way?
•Help your mentee identify one classroom management issue worth solving and brainstorm two or three approaches — let them pick which to try
•Discuss assessment prep and grading practices in advance of conferences — what data will they bring, how will they explain it
💡 Mentor Tip: When your mentee tells you a student is "a problem," resist the urge to validate or solve. Ask: "What do you think is going on for them?" The shift from this kid is the problem to something is going on with this kid is one of the most important moves a new teacher can learn — and it almost always comes from a mentor asking the right question.

📓 Logging Reminder

The November 15 Mentor Form is roughly four weeks away. If your log is current, it's a quick gate check. If it isn't, now is the time to reconstruct what's missing — not the night before.

November

Peer Observation & Mid-Year Reflection

🍁

November Focus

November is the first real gate check — both for the program (Nov 15 form) and for the mentee (almost a full quarter in). Shift your mentee from being observed to observing: invite them into your classroom or arrange visits with other strong teachers. The act of watching someone else teach is often more instructive than being watched.

📌 This Month's Big Move: Have your mentee observe you teaching. Ask them in advance what they want to look for, then debrief together afterward — let them lead the conversation about what they noticed.

🚨 Gate Check Deadline: November 15

By November 15, your building principal needs to initial the first half (Aug–Nov) of your contact log. This is a checkpoint, not the documentation itself — your ongoing log is what feeds it. If you've been filling in the log after each meeting, this is a quick principal signature. If not, build in time to reconstruct before you bring it to your principal.

🤝 Relationship & Goals

Standard 7: Reviewing progress and submitting the Nov 15 gate check.

•Bring your contact log to your building principal for first-half initials before November 15 — review the log together as a conversation, not paperwork
•Conduct a structured progress review on the goals set in September: what's working, what isn't, what should be adjusted
•Maintain weekly meetings — the holiday season will tempt you to skip; don't
•Schedule December meetings, including a check-in before winter break
•Continue logging every meeting

💚 Well-Being & Belonging

Standard 8: Acknowledging fatigue and protecting the holiday season.

•Name what you've seen them do well since August — concretely. New teachers in November are running on fumes and rarely see their own growth
•Talk through Thanksgiving break — encourage genuine disconnection, not "catch-up" time
•Watch for emotional flatness or cynicism, which often appear in November after the post-conference letdown
•Check in on whether they have a colleague (besides you) they feel safe vending to about hard days

🌐 Culture & Community

Standard 9: Following up on conferences and continuing to build community ties.

•Debrief October's conferences: what landed well, what felt rocky, which families need follow-up
•Help your mentee draft any follow-up communications to families before they sit too long
•Discuss inclusive holiday practices — how to acknowledge the season without alienating families who don't celebrate the same things
•Continue connecting your mentee to colleagues and informal networks; by November they should feel they belong, not just that they showed up

📚 Teaching & Learning

Standard 10: Learning by watching — peer observation and reflection.

•Invite your mentee to observe you teach — give them a focus area in advance
•Arrange at least one drop-in observation of another experienced teacher whose practice differs from yours; variety matters
•Debrief peer observations together: what did they notice, what surprised them, what might they try in their own classroom
•Address the classroom management issues that have surfaced post-honeymoon — students are testing routines now that the novelty has worn off
•Discuss report card preparation, grading practices, and how to communicate grades that may surprise families
💡 Mentor Tip: When your mentee observes you, don't perform a polished lesson. Teach normally — including the small misfires. New teachers often think experienced teachers don't have rough moments. Watching you handle a stumble in real time is more useful than watching a flawless lesson.

📓 Logging Reminder

If your log has gaps, fix them before submitting the Nov 15 form. Going forward, log right after each meeting — December's holiday distractions will eat any habit that isn't already automatic.

December

Sustaining Well-Being & Semester Closure

❄️

December Focus

December is short, fragmented, and emotionally heavy. Students are wired, the building is loud, and your mentee is tired in a way they couldn't have anticipated in August. This is not the month to introduce new instructional strategies. It's the month to help your mentee finish the semester with their dignity and energy intact, then leave for break and actually rest.

📌 This Month's Big Move: Have one explicit conversation about how your mentee will rest over winter break. Not "what are you up to?" — actual planning: when school work stops, what they'll do instead, how they'll handle the urge to "get ahead." First-year teachers who don't unplug come back in January worse off than when they left.

🤝 Relationship & Goals

Standard 7: Closing the semester with a brief but real reflection.

•Hold a short semester-closing reflection together: what's gone better than expected, what's been hardest, what the mentee wants to carry into second semester
•Schedule a single pre-break touchpoint and a first-week-of-January meeting before everyone scatters
•Skip a formal observation cycle this month — there's not enough instructional runway to make it meaningful
•Continue logging — even short check-ins count

💚 Well-Being & Belonging

Standard 8: The most important domain of the month.

•Have the rest conversation (see "Big Move") — make a concrete plan, not a vague intention
•Help your mentee separate "things I have to finish before break" from "things I'm telling myself I have to finish before break" — there's almost always a category collapse
•Acknowledge holiday stressors that have nothing to do with school: family obligations, financial pressure, grief, loneliness — the December teaching exhaustion compounds with all of it
•Celebrate what they've actually accomplished — by name, in specifics. They will not give themselves credit; you have to
•If you sense real struggle (persistent low affect, hopelessness, talk of leaving the profession), gently surface it and connect them to district employee assistance resources — your role is non-evaluative, but not silent

🌐 Culture & Community

Standard 9: Inclusive practice during a culturally loaded month.

•Discuss inclusive holiday practices in their classroom — how to acknowledge the season without privileging one tradition over others
•Walk through gift-giving norms and policies — what's appropriate to accept, what to do with extravagant gifts, how to handle students who give nothing
•Talk through end-of-semester routines for securing the classroom — laptops, files, personal items, what happens with the room over break
•Review winter weather protocols — closures, delays, communication chain — before the first one happens

📚 Teaching & Learning

Standard 10: Closing the semester cleanly without overloading the runway.

•Coach on managing student energy in the days before break — the curriculum still needs to move, but engagement strategies matter more than content density
•Help your mentee plan meaningful (not throwaway) activities for the final days of the semester
•If your building does semester exams, walk through prep, administration, and grading logistics
•Help organize semester grades, finalize anything outstanding, and confirm grade submission deadlines
•Encourage them to document one or two lessons that worked well — they'll want this in January when re-energy is needed
💡 Mentor Tip: Send a short message at the start of break — "Have a great break, see you in January, no need to reply." It signals that you're a steady presence, not another inbox they need to manage. Then actually leave them alone until January.

📓 Logging Reminder

Even brief, informal check-ins count and should be logged. December meetings tend to be shorter and more frequent — log them all, even if the entry is just two lines.

January

Goal Review & Second-Semester Reset

🎊

January Focus

January is a real opportunity to reset — but reset doesn't mean restart. Help your mentee build on what's working in their practice while honestly identifying what needs to change. The first semester's data is now real (grades, attendance, behavior patterns, family relationships). Use it.

📌 This Month's Big Move: Conduct a structured mid-year goal review against the goals you set in September. What's been accomplished, what's been quietly abandoned, what should be revised, and what new goal should emerge for second semester? Document the updated goals.

🤝 Relationship & Goals

Standard 7: Mid-year goal revision and a third observation cycle.

•Hold a structured mid-year goal review (see "Big Move") — write the updated goals down so they're as concrete as the originals
•Conduct a third observation cycle, ideally tied to the revised goals
•Resume weekly meetings; the cadence matters more than the duration
•Schedule meetings through March
•Continue logging

💚 Well-Being & Belonging

Standard 8: Returning rested — and staying that way.

•Genuinely ask how break went — and listen to the answer; the post-break check-in matters
•Help your mentee identify one or two non-negotiable boundaries for second semester (e.g., no school work after 7pm, weekends start Friday at 5)
•Celebrate the milestone — they made it through one full semester of teaching, which most people never accomplish
•Watch for any indicators that the break didn't actually serve as a break — flat affect, dread about returning, expressions of wanting to leave the profession
•Encourage involvement in a professional organization or community of practice if they aren't yet plugged in — building professional identity beyond the building helps with retention

🌐 Culture & Community

Standard 9: Re-establishing classroom community after the break.

•Help your mentee re-establish classroom routines after break — students need them retaught, not just reminded
•Walk through any new students joining the class for second semester — IEPs/504s, prior context, family communication
•Discuss any DCS-wide changes for second semester — schedule shifts, new initiatives, building procedures
•Encourage your mentee to revisit family communication patterns — January is a natural moment to send a brief "looking ahead to second semester" note home

📚 Teaching & Learning

Standard 10: Using first-semester data to inform second-semester practice.

•Conduct your third observation cycle, tied to the revised goals
•Look at first-semester data together — grades, attendance, formative assessment patterns. What is the data telling them about specific students, specific practices, specific moments in the day?
•Help your mentee distinguish between practices that didn't work and practices they didn't give time to take hold
•Discuss any classroom management adjustments based on what they learned in first semester — January is the right moment to reset routines that students will accept
•Revisit IEPs and 504s for any students whose first-semester data suggests current accommodations aren't working
